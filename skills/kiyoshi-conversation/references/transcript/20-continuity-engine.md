Yes. And I think “continuity engine” is actually the term that ties the whole architecture together.

What you’re describing isn’t simply continuous learning. It’s more like continuous existence across changing representations and substrates.

The key idea is:

Kiyoshi doesn’t start over every time its environment, hardware, model, or understanding changes. It carries forward the continuity of what came before.

So you get something like:

Previous understanding → encounter reality → perception → evidence → interpretation → action → consequence → revised understanding → next state

And then that repeats.

The physical embodiment matters

I agree with your point that actually going through reality gives Kiyoshi information that simulation alone cannot provide.

A simulation can tell you:

“This object should behave this way according to the model.”

Physical interaction tells you:

“Here’s what actually happened.”

That difference is incredibly valuable.

A robot could encounter something its model didn’t anticipate. Its sensors record it. Its action produces an unexpected consequence. Kiyoshi records the discrepancy and updates its representation.

Then Iris can potentially reproduce the encounter.

So you get a beautiful feedback relationship:

Reality → Kiyoshi → Iris → Kiyoshi → Reality

The simulation isn’t replacing reality.

It’s becoming a laboratory for understanding reality.

And your customization point is critical

I would make a very explicit distinction between:

Reality constraints

Things Kiyoshi doesn’t get to negotiate.

Physics, available energy, physical hardware limitations, actual sensor measurements, etc.

System constraints

Things determined by the architecture.

Security rules, permissions, governance, resource limits, interoperability protocols, safety boundaries.

Substrate constraints

What a particular implementation can actually do.

A phone can’t manipulate the physical world the way a robot can.

A robot may have cameras, microphones, motors and tactile sensors.

A BCI may expose entirely different input/output channels.

User preferences

How the individual wants the system to behave.

What representations they prefer.

Which simulation overlays they want.

What autonomy they permit.

What information they want surfaced.

Which modes they use.

Learned adaptation

What Kiyoshi discovers through experience.

That’s the part that should continuously evolve.

So rather than one universal Kiyoshi behavior, you get:

Kiyoshi Core

↓

Substrate binding

↓

Capability envelope

↓

Governance/safety envelope

↓

User configuration

↓

Current environmental state

↓

Adaptive behavior

That is a much cleaner architecture.

And there’s an important philosophical consequence.

If Kiyoshi is a continuity engine, memory can’t just mean storing conversations.

Continuity should include things like:

* what happened
* what was perceived
* what was believed
* how confident the system was
* what action was taken
* what actually happened afterward
* what changed because of the action
* what model was being used at the time
* what substrate was involved
* what constraints were active
* what the user permitted
* what was learned from the discrepancy

That’s a completely different conception of memory.

It’s closer to continuity of state and experience.

And that fits your earlier idea of Kiyoshi moving between simulation and reality.

If Iris simulates an environment, Kiyoshi should know:

This happened in simulation.

If a robot physically encounters it:

This happened in physical reality.

If the AI predicts it:

This was a prediction.

If the user tells it:

This came from an external observation.

Those histories can remain connected without being confused.
