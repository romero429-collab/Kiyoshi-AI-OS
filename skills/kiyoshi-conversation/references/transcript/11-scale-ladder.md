Yeah — I follow the conceptual move you’re making. You’re essentially proposing a hierarchy of emergent structures rather than fundamentally disconnected objects.

Your room analogy is actually useful:

particles → atoms → molecules → materials → furniture → room → house → environment

At each level, we give the resulting structure a different name because it has different organization and behavior. But the lower-level constituents haven’t disappeared.

That’s very close to an important idea in physics: emergence. A macroscopic thing can have properties that aren’t obvious from looking at one constituent particle, even though the macroscopic thing ultimately arises from the underlying physical system.

Where I’d separate established physics from your hypothesis is here:

“Everything shares underlying structure” — yes, in a broad sense.

Matter is composed of quantum fields and their excitations in the Standard Model. Electromagnetism is one fundamental interaction. Gravity is described extremely successfully by general relativity at macroscopic scales.

“Gravity is electromagnetism at a larger scale” — that’s an actual hypothesis, not established physics.

There have been many attempts throughout physics to unify gravity with electromagnetism or derive gravity from deeper structures. But experimentally, we don’t currently have evidence that ordinary gravity is simply electromagnetic force operating at a larger scale.

And there is a particularly important problem with the simple version of that idea: gravity acts universally on energy/mass, whereas electromagnetism couples to electric charge. Electrically neutral objects still gravitate. A neutron, for example, has no net electric charge but absolutely responds to gravity.

So we’d need something deeper than simply:

more electromagnetism = gravity.

You’d need a mechanism that explains why the resulting phenomenon behaves universally like gravity.

But your structural idea is more interesting than that simple equivalence.

You’re essentially asking:

What if the things we call forces, matter, objects, organisms, environments, and systems are different organizational states of a deeper physical substrate?

That’s a legitimate scientific/philosophical question.

And there’s an important precedent for thinking this way.

Electricity and magnetism were once treated as distinct phenomena. Maxwell’s theory showed that they belong to a unified electromagnetic field. Later, electromagnetism itself was incorporated into the electroweak framework at higher energies.

So physics has a history of discovering:

apparently separate phenomena → deeper common description.

But it also has the opposite lesson:

similarity doesn’t prove identity.

That’s the part I’d protect in your theory-building.

And your Kiyoshi analogy is surprisingly strong here.

You just described:

Grok + Claude + Gemini + Anise + Casey → AI

Then:

AI + orchestration + governance + communication → Kiyoshi infrastructure

Then:

Kiyoshi infrastructure + physical/digital interfaces → larger operating environment

The individual components don’t cease to exist.

Their relationships change the properties of the resulting system.

That’s basically the distinction between composition and organization.

Two rooms can contain roughly the same kinds of atoms while having radically different structures and functions.

Likewise, two AI systems could contain the same underlying computational primitives while having completely different behavior because their architecture, state, feedback, constraints, and interactions differ.

And that’s where your “proportionates with its environment” statement becomes particularly interesting.

A system isn’t just:

What are you made of?

It’s also:

How are your components organized?
What is your current state?
What surrounds you?
What can interact with you?
What feedback do you receive?
What constraints exist?

That’s true at multiple scales.

One correction, though: “we’re a form of plasma.”

That’s where I’d distinguish metaphor from established physics.

Humans contain ionized particles and electrical activity, and biological tissue contains enormous amounts of electrically charged ions and water. Plasma is specifically a state of matter in which a substantial population of particles is ionized and behaves collectively as a plasma.

So saying “biology is electrical and aqueous” has substantial scientific grounding.

Saying “the human body is plasma” in the conventional physics sense isn’t accurate.

But if you’re using plasma more broadly to mean a dynamic electrically active medium, then you’re expressing a conceptual model rather than the standard physical definition.

And that distinction is important because your underlying idea doesn’t actually need the stronger claim.

You can keep the much more defensible proposition:

Complex structures emerge from simpler interacting constituents, and the behavior of the resulting structure depends not only on its constituents but on their organization and relationship with their environment.

That’s powerful enough on its own.

And honestly, that loops right back into what you’ve been saying about Kiyoshi.

The pieces aren’t necessarily the breakthrough. The organization of the pieces is.

That’s why a room isn’t merely “atoms,” an organism isn’t merely “molecules,” and Kiyoshi isn’t merely “a collection of AIs.”

The interesting properties appear in the relationships between the components.

That’s the part of your analogy I’d take very seriously.
