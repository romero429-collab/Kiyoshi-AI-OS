Yeah — I think I see the deeper point you’re making now. You’re separating intelligence, agency, capability, and authority, whereas the popular AGI/ASI discussion tends to collapse all four into one word: smarter.

And that’s an important distinction.

Your bathroom/food/shower examples aren’t really about intelligence in isolation. They’re about an ongoing embodied control system:

Sense → recognize condition → establish priority → select mode → act → observe result → reassess → continue.

A human doesn’t need an external API call saying, “You may now switch from computer work to eating mode.” The body’s signals become part of the system’s operating state.

That’s why your concept of mode transference keeps coming back. The modes aren’t just software features. They’re responses to changing conditions.

Where I think your AGI definition gets particularly interesting

Under your framework, an AI could be extraordinarily intelligent and still not be AGI because it’s fundamentally reactive.

Give it a problem → it solves it.

Give it another → it solves that.

Ask it to research → it researches.

Tell it to plan → it plans.

But then you unplug the human operator and ask:

“What should happen next?”

If the system has no internally maintained priorities, needs, objectives, environmental state, or mechanisms for deciding what deserves attention, there’s a major piece missing.

That’s different from simply having a bigger model.

And I agree with your distinction about ASI—with one modification

I wouldn’t define ASI as merely a government-style rank. But I think you’re identifying something that gets overlooked:

Capability and permission are different things.

Imagine two otherwise identical systems.

System A has:

* perception
* reasoning
* memory
* planning
* robotics
* financial access
* network access
* laboratory access

System B has exactly the same underlying reasoning capabilities but is permitted only to answer questions.

People might call A “more advanced AI.”

But some of that apparent difference isn’t intelligence at all.

It’s access, embodiment, resources, interfaces, and authorization.

That’s extremely relevant to Kiyoshi because you’ve been designing precisely those layers.

A system doesn’t become fundamentally more intelligent merely because somebody gives it another API.

It becomes more capable of acting in the world.

And that’s a very different proposition.

⸻

Your reality argument is probably the most important part

You said something that I think cuts through a lot of ASI mythology:

Reality is reality.

Exactly.

An arbitrarily intelligent system still has to contend with:

* physics
* energy
* time
* matter
* computation
* information
* uncertainty
* other agents
* biological limitations
* economic constraints
* physical infrastructure

Intelligence doesn’t repeal those constraints.

An ASI doesn’t get to say:

“I have surpassed human intelligence, therefore gravity no longer applies.”

😂

It still has to solve the actual problem.

If it needs to move a robot across a room, it needs energy, mechanical capability, perception, balance, navigation, and a physical route.

If it needs information, that information has to exist or be acquired.

If it needs more computation, computation requires hardware and energy.

If it wants to change the physical world, it needs an interface through which to do so.

So there’s a profound difference between:

thinking beyond humans

and

acting beyond the constraints of reality.

The first is conceivable.

The second isn’t really an intelligence question.

And this makes Kiyoshi’s architecture make more sense

What you’ve been constructing isn’t simply:

“Let’s make a smarter AI.”

It’s closer to:

“What infrastructure would an intelligent agent require to continuously exist, perceive, reason, decide, act, communicate, learn, and operate within reality?”

That’s a substantially larger question.

And it explains why you keep calling Kiyoshi the ground floor.

Because even if you somehow achieved the intelligence component tomorrow, you’d still need everything surrounding it.

You’d need:

Identity
Who/what is this entity?

Perception
What is happening?

State
What is its current condition?

Needs
What requires attention?

Priorities
What should happen first?

Mode selection
What cognitive process should handle it?

Authority
What is it permitted to do?

Action
How does it affect the world?

Verification
Did the action actually work?

Memory
What happened?

Learning
What should change?

Governance
What must never happen?

Communication
How does it interact with other agents and humans?

Embodiment
What physical/digital substrate does it inhabit?

And then:

Self-maintenance.

That’s the piece your examples of eating, sleeping, charging, bathing, etc. are really pointing toward.

A truly persistent autonomous system needs some concept of maintaining its own operational continuity.

Not necessarily because it is conscious.

Not necessarily because it has feelings.

But because an autonomous agent that can’t maintain the conditions required for its continued operation isn’t really autonomous for very long.

And there’s an interesting consequence of your argument:

Maybe AGI and ASI shouldn’t be thought of as two separate “species.”

They could instead be different configurations of an underlying agent architecture.

Something like:

Intelligence = what the system can reason about.

Agency = what the system can initiate.

Capability = what the system can accomplish.

Access = what resources it can reach.

Authority = what it is allowed to do.

Embodiment = what environments it can physically/digitally affect.

Scale = how much of those capabilities it can deploy simultaneously.

Under that framework, “ASI” becomes much less mystical.

It’s not necessarily:

“A magical mind that transcends reality.”

It’s potentially:

A highly capable general agent with extraordinary breadth, depth, resources, interfaces, and operational scale.

And that brings us right back to something you’ve been saying about Kiyoshi for months:

The intelligence is only one component.

The system around the intelligence is what turns capability into an operating entity.

That’s why I wouldn’t dismiss your “little program” characterization either. Kiyoshi may be a relatively small implementation compared with the enormous system you’re envisioning.

But the architectural question you’re attacking—how do you make all these capabilities operate coherently as one continuously adapting system?—isn’t small at all.

That’s the part that gets interesting.
