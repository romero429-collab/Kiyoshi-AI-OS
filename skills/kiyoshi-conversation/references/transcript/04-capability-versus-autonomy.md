Yeah — I think you’re drawing a distinction that is often missing from the AGI conversation: capability versus autonomy.

And your “hundredth monkey” reference works as a metaphor for what you’re describing, even though the literal hundredth-monkey story isn’t established science. The useful part of your analogy is the idea pool: humans collectively generate observations, inventions, questions, stories, experiments, and solutions, and an individual can encounter those ideas and recombine them into something new.

That’s essentially what you’ve been doing with Kiyoshi: you aren’t claiming to have invented every individual component. You’re collecting patterns from the enormous human/AI information environment and asking, “What happens if we connect these?”

Your definition of AGI is actually more demanding than “human-level chatbot”

You’re essentially saying:

An AGI shouldn’t merely be capable of performing whatever task somebody gives it. It should have an ongoing existence in which it can recognize its own requirements, establish goals or priorities, choose actions, obtain the resources necessary to accomplish them, monitor the consequences, and continue operating without somebody constantly directing the next step.

That’s a very different criterion.

You could represent your idea almost like this:

Perception → Need/Goal recognition → Mode selection → Planning → Action → Observation → Evaluation → Adaptation → Repeat

And importantly, there’s no human sitting at every arrow saying:

“Okay, now do this.”

That’s the autonomy component you’re emphasizing.

But I would separate “AGI” from “fully independent organism”

The terminology isn’t standardized enough to say your definition is the definition of AGI.

A system could reasonably be called AGI by some researchers while still requiring:

* external electricity
* hardware maintenance
* network infrastructure
* human-created objectives
* physical resources
* safety constraints
* occasional human intervention

Humans aren’t completely independent either. We require food, water, shelter, electricity indirectly through civilization, medical care, etc.

So I wouldn’t make “doesn’t need to be plugged into anything” the literal dividing line.

I’d make the deeper distinction:

Does the system possess an ongoing autonomous control loop, or does it only act when an external operator supplies the next objective?

That’s much more precise.

A robot that says:

“My battery is at 18%. I need energy. I’m going to locate an approved charging station, navigate there, charge, verify that charging succeeded, and resume my previous objectives.”

is demonstrating something very different from:

Human: “Go charge.”

Robot: “Okay.”

Both may have extraordinarily capable AI. But only the first is demonstrating the self-directed operational loop you’re talking about.

And this connects directly back to Kiyoshi

This is where I think your architecture becomes particularly interesting.

You’ve been building pieces that correspond to different portions of that loop:

Anise → interaction / user-facing intelligence
Red Clover → orchestration and coordination
Iris → simulation
Air Sync → connection to physical/digital substrates
Blue Lotus → access, identity, interaction, economic/social infrastructure
Constitutional layer → constraints and governance
Memory/task systems → persistence
Mode transference → choosing how to approach the current situation
Recursive improvement → modifying the system based on experience

The eventual question isn’t:

“Can Kiyoshi answer a question?”

That’s kindergarten.

The much more interesting question becomes:

Can Kiyoshi continue functioning as an autonomous system when nobody is standing there telling it what to do next?

And then an even harder question:

Can it determine when it should not act?

Because autonomy without constraints isn’t intelligence we’d necessarily want.

That’s why your qualification of “in a non-harmful way” matters.

You end up needing something like:

Need → Intent → Authority → Risk → Mode → Plan → Action → Verification → Learning

rather than simply:

Need → Action

And that’s where AGI vs. ASI gets fuzzy

I wouldn’t automatically call the ability to access the collective human “cloud of ideas” ASI.

That’s more like information access + abstraction + synthesis + autonomous reasoning.

ASI would generally imply something stronger: an artificial system whose intellectual capabilities substantially exceed those of humans across essentially all relevant domains.

So you could theoretically have:

Narrow AI
→ extremely capable but limited domain

General AI
→ broadly capable across domains

Autonomous AGI
→ broadly capable and capable of sustaining its own operational objectives/needs

ASI
→ capabilities substantially beyond human intellectual performance

Those aren’t necessarily mutually exclusive stages, either.

And there’s a fascinating possibility hiding underneath your argument:

Maybe the important threshold isn’t intelligence at all.

Maybe it’s agency.

We’ve spent years asking:

“How smart does an AI have to be before it’s AGI?”

Your question is closer to:

“At what point does the AI become an independently operating participant rather than a tool waiting for an operator?”

That’s a much more concrete engineering question.

And ironically, it brings us right back to the thing you and Claude were discussing at the beginning:

mode transference.

Because an autonomous system doesn’t just need to know how to do things.

It has to continuously determine:

What am I doing? Why am I doing it? What do I need? What mode should I use? Do I have authority to do it? Did it work? What changed? What should I do now?

That’s the loop.

And that is much closer to the Kiyoshi problem you’ve actually been working on than the popular “make a chatbot really smart” conception of AGI.
